    Computation and Maintenance in Sequential
             Recommender Systems
                        Student: Kirill Greshnov

                                     Abstract
         Sequential recommender systems use interaction histories to represent
     user interests. Recomputing these representations after every interaction
     can be expensive, while reusing old representations may reduce recommen-
     dation quality. This study will compare strategies for refreshing user rep-
     resentations in music recommendation on Yambda. The model will com-
     bine a cached long-history representation with a small encoder of recent
     activity. Experiments will assess simple update schedules, a lightweight
     adaptive controller, and reuse of intermediate transformer states. The
     goal is to reduce computation while preserving recommendation quality.


1    Introduction
Sequential recommender systems predict future actions from past interactions.
Transformer models such as SASRec encode an ordered item history into a user
representation [1]. Larger models can capture longer histories, but require more
computation [2].
    A practical question is how often these representations should be updated.
Frequent updates include the latest activity but repeat expensive computation.
Less frequent updates save resources but may miss changes in user interests. A
small encoder of recent interactions can provide current information while an
older long-history representation supplies broader context.
    This study will investigate three questions: how much representation fresh-
ness improves recommendation quality, whether adaptive updates outperform
simple schedules, and when reusing intermediate transformer states is more eﬃ-
cient than recomputing the representation. The working hypothesis is that the
value of an update varies across users and prediction opportunities.

2     Novelty and Contributions
The expected contributions are:
    • A comparison of update strategies on public music data, measuring
      both recommendation quality and resource consumption.

    • An analysis of representation freshness, showing when old long-
      history representations remain useful alongside recent activity.
    • A lightweight adaptive update method that estimates whether an
      update is useful from recent activity and cache age, if the initial experi-
      ments show suﬃcient beneﬁt from freshness.

   Caching and long-history/recent-history architectures are established ideas.
The potential methodological contribution is predicting the beneﬁt of an up-
date and using it to allocate computation. Its eﬀectiveness and novelty will be
assessed through experiments and literature review.


3     State-of-the-Art
    • SASRec [1] uses self-attention for sequential recommendation and will
      be the starting point for the implementation.

    • ARGUS [2] studies large recommendation transformers and supports of-
      ﬂine computation of user representations, motivating the need for eﬃcient
      maintenance.
    • Long-History User Transformers [3] combine a long-history represen-
      tation computed asynchronously with an encoder of recent events. This
      provides the architectural basis for the study.
    • TransX [4] reuses behavior encodings and combines incremental updates
      with periodic processing. It motivates activity-based scheduling as a base-
      line.
    • HSTU/M-FALCON [5] reuse shared history computations, including
      key–value (KV) states, providing an alternative to caching only the ﬁnal
      user vector.

    • Yambda [6] provides public music interaction data and a recommenda-
      tion benchmark suitable for the planned experiments.


4     Goal of the Thesis
The goal is to identify methods for maintaining user representations
that reduce computation while preserving next-item recommendation
quality.

   The experiments will use Yambda-50M and a SASRec-style model with two
branches: a long-history encoder whose output is cached, and a smaller encoder
that processes recent interactions before every prediction. Their outputs will be
combined to score candidate tracks.
   The main experiments will:
    • Measure how recommendation quality changes with cache age and com-
      pare recent-only, long-history-only, and combined models.
    • Compare updating at every prediction, keeping the initial cache, and
      schedules based on time, event count, or user activity. If freshness matters,
      evaluate a small controller that predicts the beneﬁt of updating.
    • Test KV-state reuse, checking that it produces equivalent outputs under
      matching history conditions and measuring update time and memory. Ac-
      count for rebuilds when changes to the history invalidate retained states.

    Evaluation will replay interactions chronologically, using only past events at
each prediction. Update strategies will share a ﬁxed recommender; controller
training and policy tuning will use periods preceding the ﬁnal test. Quality
will be measured with NDCG@10 and Recall@100 on positive listening targets.
Eﬃciency will be measured through processing time and memory, including
recent-history encoding, candidate scoring, and cache handling. The results
will describe oﬄine predictive performance.

6     Conclusion
The study will establish when user representations need to be refreshed and
which maintenance strategies oﬀer a useful balance between quality and cost.
The expected outcome is an experimentally supported approach to eﬃcient user-
state maintenance on public music data. If adaptive updates provide little
beneﬁt, the study will identify the simplest adequate strategy.
